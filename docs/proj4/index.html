Image Warping and Mosaicing

One photo can only fit so much of a scene. For this project I took overlapping photos around campus and joined them into larger images called mosaics. The challenge is that turning the camera changes how the scene looks, so I can't just place the photos next to each other. I first find how matching features move between photos, reshape the images until those features line up, and blend the overlap to hide the seams.

Part A

Image Warping and Mosaicing

A.1

Shoot the Pictures

To join two photos, they need to overlap, meaning part of the scene shows up in both so I can use it to line them up. I kept the phone in the same spot and only turned it between shots. That matters because if the camera moves sideways, nearby objects shift more than faraway ones, and then no single transform can line both of them up. I also took each set quickly so people and lighting wouldn't change much in between. At Haas Pavilion and on Memorial Glade facing Doe Library I turned from left to right, and in the eucalyptus grove I tilted upward, so those photos go from the ground up to the treetops.

A.2

Recover Homographies

The first step is to describe where a feature in one photo should land in the next. For example, a window corner might appear near the right edge of the left photo and near the left edge of the next photo. Those two locations are a matching pair, also called a correspondence. I record each location as pixel coordinates (x, y), with x increasing to the right and y increasing downward.

Now I want one transform that moves every point in one photo onto its match in the other. Because the camera only turned, a projective transform, also called a homography, can do this. It's a 3×3 matrix H, which I solve for using the matching points.

A.2 Setting up the equations

To apply H to a point (x, y), I first write the point in homogeneous coordinates by adding a 1, giving (x, y, 1), so H can be applied with one matrix multiplication. That gives three numbers, and dividing the first two by the third brings the point back to regular pixel coordinates (x', y'). Written out, that gives the formulas below (the bottom-right entry of H only sets the overall scale, so I fix it to 1, which leaves eight unknowns, a through h).

\[ H = \begin{bmatrix}a & b & c \\ d & e & f \\ g & h & 1\end{bmatrix}, \qquad x'=\frac{ax+by+c}{gx+hy+1},\qquad y'=\frac{dx+ey+f}{gx+hy+1} \]

For each matching pair I know (x, y) and (x', y'), so multiplying both sides of each formula by the bottom of the fraction and rearranging gives two equations that are linear in the unknowns:

\[ \begin{aligned} ax+by+c-gxx'-hyx' &= x' \\ dx+ey+f-gxy'-hyy' &= y' \end{aligned} \]

Since each pair gives two equations, I need at least four pairs. My computeH(im1_pts, im2_pts) stacks the equations from every pair into one system, where \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\) holds a through h:

\[ A\boldsymbol{\theta}=\mathbf{b},\qquad \boldsymbol{\theta}=\begin{bmatrix}a&b&c&d&e&f&g&h\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf T} \]

With more than four pairs, small clicking errors mean no single H fits every equation exactly, so I use least squares, which finds the H that fits all the pairs as closely as possible overall.

A.2 Correspondences

For every pair of neighboring photos I clicked matching points across the overlap, where the same number marks the same spot in both photos. I picked sharp corners that are easy to spot in both photos, like window frames, banner edges, the roofline, and knots and forks on the tree trunks, and spread them out so the fit holds across the whole overlap instead of just one area.

A.3

Warp the Images

A.3 Inverse warping

H tells me where each point moves, but to make a new image I need a color for every pixel in it. The obvious way is to push each original pixel forward through H, but when the image gets stretched, some output pixels never receive anything and are left as holes. So I go backward instead. I push the four corners through H to see how big the output needs to be, and then for every output pixel I use the inverse, \(H^{-1}\), to find where it came from in the original photo, so every output pixel gets filled. Some output pixels land outside the original photo and have nothing to show, so I also keep an alpha mask, which is 1 where the pixel came from the photo and 0 where it's empty. The mosaic uses it later so the empty black areas don't get blended in.

The source location may be something like (100.3, 80.7), between pixels rather than at a stored color. Interpolation means estimating the color at that location, and I wrote two versions from scratch. warpImageNearestNeighbor rounds to the closest pixel. warpImageBilinear takes the four pixels around the point and blends them, giving more weight to whichever ones are closer.

A.3 Rectification

Before joining photos, I tested the warp on a simpler problem: making an angled view of a flat rectangle look straight on. This is rectification. I clicked four corners on the walkway and on one flat section of the Valley Life Sciences Building (VLSB), then used computeH to map those corners to an upright rectangle. The result looks like a view from straight above the walkway or straight in front of VLSB.

A.3 Nearest neighbor vs. bilinear

At full size the two results look almost the same, so here is a close-up of the same spot in each. Nearest neighbor is faster since it reads just one pixel, but edges come out jagged and blocky because neighboring output pixels jump between whole source pixels. Bilinear takes a weighted average of the four nearest pixels, which takes a little more than twice as long, but the brick lines and window frames stay smooth. The extra time is small next to the better quality, so I used bilinear for everything after this.

A.4

Blend the Images into a Mosaic

A.4 Procedure

Rectification warps a photo into a view I choose myself. A mosaic uses the same warp, but the target view is one of the photos. For Doe and the grove, I keep the middle photo as it is and warp the other two into its view, since the middle one overlaps both and keeps the stretching about even on each side. My homographies go from each photo to the next one, so for the left photo I use H as it is, and for the right photo I use its inverse to go backward into the middle photo. For Haas there are only two photos, so the right one stays put. Pushing every photo's corners through its H tells me how big the final canvas needs to be to fit everything.

A.4 Reducing edge artifacts

Each warped photo covers part of the canvas, shown by its alpha mask. Where several photos cover the same pixel, I choose the one whose warped center is closest, which gives each photo a hard mask showing which pixels to take from it. Below are the middle Doe photo's coverage and hard mask, with white for 1 and black for 0, followed by the mosaic made by applying all three hard masks.

The hard masks put the photos together, but switching directly between them can leave brightness seams, so I use two-band blending from class to smooth the transitions. I get the blend weights in two steps.

  1. Apply a Gaussian blur to the hard selection masks to soften transitions between photos.
  2. Fade the weights toward zero near each warped photo's original alpha-mask border, where its valid pixels end, so the contribution fades out gradually instead of cutting off suddenly at its borders.

I use these weights to average the low-frequency colors. For pixel \(p\), the blend of two photos is given below, where L is a photo's low-frequency color and w is its soft weight. The same sum extends to three photos.

\[ \text{For pixel }p,\qquad L_{\mathrm{blend}}(p)= \frac{w_A(p)\,L_A(p)+w_B(p)\,L_B(p)}{w_A(p)+w_B(p)} \]

Where photos overlap, their low-frequency colors contribute according to these weights, and where only one photo covers the pixel, dividing by its weight leaves its color unchanged. I add back the high-frequency detail selected by the hard masks, so a pixel covered by only one photo keeps that photo's original color. Pixels with no coverage stay empty, and no colors are extended beyond a photo's boundary.

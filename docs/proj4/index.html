Image Warping and Mosaicing

One photo can only fit so much of a scene. For this project I took overlapping photos around campus and joined them into larger images called mosaics. The challenge is that turning the camera changes how the scene looks, so I can't just place the photos next to each other. I first find how matching features move between photos, reshape the images until those features line up, and blend the overlap to hide the seams.

Part A

A.1

Shoot the Pictures

To join two photos, they need to overlap, meaning part of the scene shows up in both so I can use it to line them up. I kept the phone in the same spot and only turned it between shots. That matters because if the camera moves sideways, nearby objects shift more than faraway ones, and then no single projective transform can line both of them up. I also took each set quickly so people and lighting wouldn't change much in between. At Haas Pavilion and on Memorial Glade facing Doe Library I turned from left to right, and in the eucalyptus grove I tilted upward, so those photos go from the ground up to the treetops.

A.2

Recover Homographies

The first step is to describe where a feature in one photo should land in the next. For example, a window corner might appear near the right edge of the left photo and near the left edge of the next photo. Those two locations are a matching pair, also called a correspondence. I record each location as pixel coordinates (x, y), with x increasing to the right and y increasing downward.

Now I want one transform that moves every point in one photo onto its match in the other. Since the camera only turned between shots, a projective transform, also called a homography, can do this. It's a 3×3 matrix H, and the matching points are what I use to solve for it.

A.2 Setting up the equations

H has eight numbers in it, and a point (x, y) moves to (x', y') using the formulas below. The top of each fraction shifts, rotates and stretches the point. The bottom changes across the image, so parts of the photo can shrink or grow, which is what makes the result look like a different viewing angle.

\[ H = \begin{bmatrix}a & b & c \\ d & e & f \\ g & h & 1\end{bmatrix}, \qquad x'=\frac{ax+by+c}{gx+hy+1},\qquad y'=\frac{dx+ey+f}{gx+hy+1} \]

Multiplying every entry of H by the same number cancels out in the division, so I fix its bottom-right entry to 1. That leaves eight unknowns. Rearranging the two coordinate equations makes each matching point pair give two linear equations:

\[ \begin{aligned} ax+by+c-gxx'-hyx' &= x' \\ dx+ey+f-gxy'-hyy' &= y' \end{aligned} \]

Each pair supplies two equations, so at least four well-spread pairs are needed for the eight unknowns. My computeH(im1_pts, im2_pts) puts all the pairs into one system. Here, \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\) holds the eight unknown entries of H, while A and \(\mathbf{b}\) contain the clicked coordinates:

\[ A\boldsymbol{\theta}=\mathbf{b},\qquad \boldsymbol{\theta}=\begin{bmatrix}a&b&c&d&e&f&g&h\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf T} \]

With more than four pairs, small clicking errors mean no single H fits every equation exactly, so I use least squares, which finds the H that fits all the pairs as closely as possible overall. A few badly clicked points can still pull the fit off, so careful clicking matters.

A.2 Correspondences

For every pair of neighboring photos I clicked matching points across the overlap, where the same number marks the same spot in both photos. I picked sharp corners on things that don't move, like window frames, banner edges, the roofline, and knots and forks on the tree trunks, and spread them out so the fit holds across the whole overlap instead of just one area. For Doe, I also nudged the clicked points using local image matching and dropped a few bad matches, which made the seams line up better.

A.3

Warp the Images

A.3 Inverse warping

H tells me where each point moves, but to make a new image I need a color for every pixel in it. The obvious way is to push each original pixel forward through H, but when the image gets stretched, some output pixels never receive anything and are left as holes. So I go backward instead. I push the four corners through H to see how big the output needs to be, and then for every output pixel I use the inverse, \(H^{-1}\), to find where it came from in the original photo, so every output pixel gets filled. Some output pixels land outside the original photo and have nothing to show, so I also keep an alpha mask, which is 1 where the pixel came from the photo and 0 where it's empty. The mosaic uses it later so the empty black areas don't get blended in.

The source location may be something like (100.3, 80.7), between pixels rather than at a stored color. Interpolation means estimating the color at that location, and I wrote two versions from scratch. warpImageNearestNeighbor rounds to the closest pixel. warpImageBilinear takes the four pixels around the point and blends them, giving more weight to whichever ones are closer.

A.3 Rectification

Before joining photos, I tested the warp on a simpler problem: making an angled view of a flat rectangle look straight on. This is rectification. I clicked four corners on the walkway and on one flat section of the Valley Life Sciences Building, then used computeH to map those corners to an upright rectangle. The destination gives me a view from above the walkway or in front of the wall. Features outside that plane may still look distorted, so I crop around the selected rectangle.

The destination rectangle's proportions matter too, since making every selection a square would squash or stretch what's inside it. So I gave each one a width-to-height ratio matching the object, a long thin strip for the walkway and a slightly wide rectangle for the wall. The results straighten the rows of bricks and the window frames.

A.3 Nearest neighbor vs. bilinear

At full size the two results look almost the same, so here is a close-up of the same spot in each. Nearest neighbor is faster since it reads just one pixel, but edges come out jagged and blocky because neighboring output pixels jump between whole source pixels. Bilinear reads four pixels and does a bit of math for each, which takes about twice as long, but the brick lines and window frames stay smooth. The extra time is small next to the better quality, so I used bilinear for everything after this.

A.4

Blend the Images into a Mosaic

A.4 Procedure

Rectification warps a photo into a view I choose myself. A mosaic uses the same warp, but the target view is one of the photos. For Doe and the grove, I keep the middle photo as it is and warp the other two into its view, since the middle one overlaps both and keeps the stretching about even on each side. My homographies go from each photo to the next one, so for the left photo I use H as it is, and for the right photo I use its inverse to go backward into the middle photo. For Haas there are only two photos, so the right one stays put. Pushing every photo's corners through its H tells me how big the final canvas needs to be to fit everything.

A.4 Reducing edge artifacts

Now all the photos sit on the same canvas, but where they overlap there are two possible colors for each pixel, and blending decides what to show. The easiest thing is to paste the photos on top of each other, but then the seam lands wherever the last photo's edge happens to be. The brightness changes a little between shots, and a photo's edge is also where its alignment is worst, so that cut is easy to see.

So I do the same thing as the blending in Project 2: cut, then blur the cut. First, each pixel goes to the photo whose center is closest, which gives one mask per photo that's 1 where that photo owns the pixel and 0 everywhere else. That puts the cut about halfway through each overlap, where the photos line up best. Then I blur each mask with a Gaussian, so instead of jumping from 1 to 0 at the cut it slides down gradually, and I multiply it by the photo's alpha mask so a photo never counts where it has no pixels. Each pixel becomes a weighted average of the photos with these blurred masks as the weights, so right at the cut it's half of each photo, and a little farther away it's all one photo. Outside the overlap only one photo has any weight, so that pixel just shows that photo. This hides the seam, but wherever the alignment is slightly off, the fade mixes detail from both photos, so edges look soft or doubled.

To keep the smooth fade without the softness, I split each photo into two layers, like a two-level version of the multiresolution blending from Project 2. The blurry layer holds the overall color and brightness, so I blend it with the blurred masks to hide the brightness change. The sharp layer, which is the photo minus its blurry version, holds the edges and texture, so I blend it with the hard masks instead and take it from only one photo at each pixel, which means edges never get doubled. Adding the two layers back together gives a smooth fade with sharp detail. This can't fix bad alignment, though, so a badly matched spot can still show a small jump. Here's the same spot on one of the Doe seams with each method. In the overwrite version, the right photo's edge is pasted on top of this spot, which is the cut you can see.

What I learned

The most surprising part for me was how much a single 3×3 matrix can do, from straightening a building to stitching a whole panorama. It also showed me how much the input matters. Clicking points carefully and turning the phone without moving it made a bigger difference than anything fancy in the blending.
